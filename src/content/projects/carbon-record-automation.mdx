## Context

Carbon Records is a cast reel engine. You give it a film; it works out who is in it,
and cuts a reel for each person. A reel is one whole conversation from the film, then
the song's drop landing exactly where the conversation ends, then a montage of the
character who was speaking — captioned, graded, vertical, under 90 seconds. That
three-part shape is the product, and most of the engineering exists to protect it.

It's 273 Python modules and about 65,000 lines, plus a FastAPI studio you drive it
from. I built it solo. Nothing it produces is posted automatically: every reel is
shown to me before it renders, and the identity step will not let me skip it.

## The problem

Carbon Record is a marketing tool. If you're promoting a song, a film, or a person,
the format that works is short vertical clips built around one recognisable face — and
producing those means someone sitting with the footage, finding every scene a given
character appears in, and cutting them together. For a feature with forty speaking
parts that's forty passes through the same film.

The work is mechanical but it isn't simple. It needs three things at once: knowing who
is on screen, knowing where scenes start and end, and knowing which moments are worth
keeping. Each is a different model, and none of them is reliable enough on its own to
trust unsupervised.

The second problem is that a pipeline doing all three runs for hours over
feature-length input. A multi-hour job that restarts from zero on any failure isn't
automation, it's a chore with extra steps. Most of the engineering here went into
surviving failure rather than into the models.

## Constraints

Everything here is a number from the source, not an estimate.

**No labelled cast.** Identities have to be discovered. Faces are detected and
embedded, then clustered with HDBSCAN at `min_cluster_size=4` and `min_samples=2` —
chosen because HDBSCAN infers how many clusters there are and marks outliers as noise
rather than forcing every extra into a lead.

**Fragmentation is emergent over feature length.** One person shot across two and a
half hours, in different scenes and lighting, splits into many clusters; in a short
clip they appear once and cluster once. Measured: a 150-second trailer gave 4 proposals
in 4 groups and a 12-minute excerpt gave 15 in 15, both with zero multi-cluster groups.
The Dark Knight's 194 proposals collapsed to 96 groups, with the Joker's single card
covering 19 clusters and 551 faces. No short source reproduces this, which is why some
of the review UI can only be tested on a full film.

**Stages run for hours, so the run has to survive the machine.** On the reference
film — Game of Thrones S05, 7,863 shots, 42 characters, 459 accepted face groups —
clustering took 2.24 hours, aura 7.8 hours, and bold_hooks 13.2 hours wall against
about 2.6 hours of actual work, the difference being 122 sleep events. A heartbeat
samples `(t, cpu, power)` every 15 seconds and work time is derived from the gaps,
because a frozen machine freezes the sampler too, so the gap *is* the sleep.

**Power state changes the numbers by more than the code does.** Measured 3.7
minutes per character on AC against 321 minutes per character on battery for the same
stage — 87×. macOS also schedules onto efficiency cores when unplugged, so a
CPU-second buys about half the work. CPU-seconds are comparable within a power state
and not across one.

**Every model call needs a bound.** Gemini clients get a 180-second timeout and three
attempts. All five extraction-path clients import that timeout from one module rather
than restating it. The retry earned itself immediately: 22 connection resets in one
restarted run, every one recovered.

**Roughly 10 GB of disk per film**, for the extracted clip library plus twice the
film's size while uploading.

## What I built

The diagram above is the extraction path. Underneath it:

`carbon/engine/cast/` holds the pipeline and the format in one namespace — 20 modules
and 6,921 lines taking a film to a labelled clip library, and 13 modules and 7,741
lines taking that library to a reel. Splitting them properly rewrites 151 relative
imports across 24 files, including the two largest modules in the repo, so it's
scheduled after the launch rehearsal rather than before it. The retired trailer format
is parked in its own package instead — 6 modules with no live importer, which is why
moving it couldn't break a render.

The model stack is InsightFace ArcFace on the `buffalo_l` pack for face embeddings,
served through ONNX Runtime; YOLO for person detection and tracking; PySceneDetect for
shot boundaries; Whisper for speech; and Gemini for the things that need judgement —
scene registers, song sections, track moods, and labelling the clusters.

The studio is FastAPI and uvicorn on port 8099. It supervises the render worker —
spawning it, restarting it if it dies, holding a lock so two app processes can't each
spawn one — but it does not start Redis. Without Redis, renders fall back to running
inside the web process: they work, they block requests, cancel stops working, and a
restart loses them. The studio says that on screen rather than degrading quietly.

`./start` runs six checks that used to be a human's job — the interpreter, the three
requirement files, ffmpeg and ffprobe, Redis, and whether the Gemini key is a real key
rather than the placeholder. The key is checked at submit, so a missing one fails in a
second instead of at stage six, two hours in.

The interpreter is pinned to Python 3.14, because `audioop-lts` needs 3.13 or newer and
a 3.12 venv fails with an error that names a package nobody has heard of instead of
naming the interpreter.

Correctness is gated by 185 pytest functions across 21 test files, run by a pre-commit
hook that refuses the commit on failure. Tiers 1 to 3 run in about 6 seconds; the slow
tier adds a real render and a 30-minute parity gate. `tests/COVERAGE.md` is a list of
what the suite does *not* reach, which is the more useful half — the identity review
is the least-covered thing in the repository, and it's the one human gate in the
pipeline.

## Decisions

### Absorbing scene extraction instead of maintaining a bridge

The first version wasn't one system. Scene extraction was a separate Gemini-based
cutter, and a third repository orchestrated the two — copy the film into the cutter's
input directory, run it, discover the clip-and-sidecar pairs it produced, then hand
each one to the reel engine. It worked, and it was three hundred lines of glue with
both sibling repositories' absolute paths hardcoded, which meant it ran on exactly one
machine.

I pulled the extraction into the pipeline instead. Shot detection now runs inside the
same process as everything downstream, which means it shares the checkpoint system,
the cache, and the failure handling rather than needing its own. The bridge function
still exists as a CLI entry point — `make_scene_reel()` is reachable, and its docstring
still describes a tool the pipeline no longer needs — but nothing in the main path
calls it.

The tradeoff is that the pipeline is now bigger and owns a problem it used to
delegate. That was worth it: a stage that lives outside the checkpoint system is a
stage that can't resume, and resumption is the property the whole system is built
around.

### 1. HDBSCAN over k-means

Cast identification is the stage everything downstream depends on, and there's no
answer key — identities have to be discovered from the footage.

k-means needs to be told how many clusters to find, and the number of people in a film
is exactly the thing I don't know going in. It also assigns every point to a cluster,
which means a background extra who appears in two frames gets absorbed into whichever
lead they're nearest in embedding space.

HDBSCAN infers cluster count from density and labels sparse points as noise rather
than forcing them somewhere. With `min_cluster_size=4` and `min_samples=2`, a face has
to recur before it becomes an identity at all. On the reference film that produced 459
face groups resolving to 42 characters — the gap between those numbers is mostly the
same person under different lighting, which is the failure mode I'd rather have than
the reverse.

The cost is that HDBSCAN gives me no control over how many identities come out, and
it's slower on large embedding sets. Both are acceptable when the alternative is
confidently wrong casting.

### 2. A human review gate over a confidence threshold

Every identity downstream depends on clustering being right, and clustering is the
least certain stage in the pipeline.

The obvious answer is a confidence threshold — accept clusters above some score,
discard the rest. I don't think a threshold is a decision, though. It's a guess about
where a model stops being trustworthy, and it's wrong in both directions: it accepts
confident mistakes and discards correct low-confidence output. Whichever number I
picked would be defensible and unfalsifiable at the same time.

So low-confidence clusters are held for a person to name. It's the only manual step in
the pipeline and it's manual on purpose. Every other stage can fail and retry; this one
can be wrong in a way retrying doesn't fix, so it's the one place a person is worth the
interruption.

It doesn't scale. One decision per job is fine for one film and a bottleneck for a
hundred. That's a real limitation, not a phase.

### 3. Deleting a stage rather than tuning it

There was a caching stage meant to make repeat runs cheaper, and a purity scan meant
to catch mixed clusters.

I measured both against what they were supposed to save. The caching stage cost 312.7
minutes to save 4.5 — off by a factor of twelve from the estimate that justified
building it. The purity scan was out by 3.4×. The instinct in both cases was to tune:
adjust the batch size, narrow the scan, make it cheaper.

I deleted them instead. A stage that costs more than it saves is not a tuning problem,
it's a stage that shouldn't exist, and tuning it would have meant spending more time
defending the decision to build it. Removing them made the pipeline smaller and faster
in the same commit.

### 4. Bounded retries over unbounded resilience

Every LLM call in the pipeline goes through one module: three attempts, a 180-second
timeout, and the same behaviour everywhere. Five extraction clients import those
constants rather than defining their own.

The alternative is per-call tuning — longer timeouts on the expensive calls, more
retries where failure is costlier. I didn't do that, because with hundreds of calls per
job, per-call tuning means hundreds of independent decisions nobody will revisit and no
way to reason about worst-case runtime. One shared policy means a hung provider
degrades one call instead of stalling a job, and the ceiling is knowable.

## Results

I'm not going to give you a throughput number, and the reason is the interesting part.

This project measures itself, and measuring itself is how it found out that three
figures it had been quoting were wrong. Aura's call budget was quoted at about 405
calls; it was 4,725 — out by 11.7×. A caching stage was justified by an estimated 26
minutes of cost against 17 minutes saved; measured, it cost 312.7 minutes and saved
4.5, so it paid five hours to save four minutes and I deleted it. A purity scan quoted
at 919.7 seconds of a render was 273. Each of those was honest when it was written and
each kept being quoted after the code underneath it changed.

There's a worse one. The identity-resolution stage reports 22.28 hours on the
reference film. That number is permanently wrong — the span contains an interruption,
an overnight sleep and a kill, all inside a single run with no process change, so no
structural test can catch it. It's labelled `unverified_span` and it must not be
scaled down or fed to anything that averages, because a row that quietly reports a
smaller number is the same failure in the other direction.

What I did instead of fixing the number was make the next one measurable: a heartbeat
samples every 15 seconds and work time is derived from the gaps, so a frozen machine
freezes the sampler and the gap *is* the sleep. CPU alone can't do this — one stage
runs at 0.04 cores while working perfectly, so any threshold on cores calls it idle.

So the honest results are the shape of the thing, not its speed. One film has
completed under current code. Anything measured on the two earlier films was produced
by code that no longer exists. On battery, every timing above is meaningless — 3.7
minutes per character on AC against 321 on battery for the same stage.

The repository's own `STATE.md` is the list of which numbers can't be trusted, and it
exists because I got this wrong three times. Publishing a tidy figure here would be
the fourth.

## Limitations

**The review gate doesn't scale, and that's structural.** One human decision per job
is fine for one film and a bottleneck at a hundred. Nothing is exported until the
faces are named, and there is no batch mode, no delegation, and no way to approve a
run without looking at it. That's the cost of choosing a person over a threshold, and
it isn't a phase I'm planning my way out of.

**A job resumes on the machine it failed on.** Checkpoints live in an
`_extraction_checkpoints` directory under the film's export root, and the shot
embedding cache is a `.npz` file beside it — both local paths. So resumption works,
but only here. Moving a half-finished film to another machine means copying the
checkpoint tree with it, and nothing in the code does that for you.

**Clustering assumes the face is there to be seen.** Detections are filtered through a
quality gate on box area and detector confidence before they reach HDBSCAN, so a
character shot from behind, in heavy shadow, or under costume either fragments across
clusters or lands in noise and never becomes an identity. The reference film's 459
accepted face groups resolving to 42 characters is the visible half of that; the
invisible half is whoever never cleared the gate.

**Without Redis the studio still works, and works worse.** Renders fall back to
running inside the web process: they block requests, cancel stops working, and a
restart loses them. The studio says so on screen rather than degrading quietly, which
is the right behaviour and not a fix.

**There is no rate limiting on model calls at all.** Each call gets three attempts and
a 180-second timeout, and that's the whole policy — the calls are issued sequentially
inside each stage, and the only coordination anywhere is a thread lock inside one
process and a file lock on the history file. Two jobs on one machine don't throttle
each other, and two app processes on different machines don't know about each other.
One film at a time hides this completely.

**One layering rule is broken on purpose and written down.**
`cast/film_index.py:46` imports a hashing helper from the web layer — the engine
reaching upward, once. Nine web modules import the engine and the engine imports the
web layer exactly there. Naming it was cheaper than moving hashing, and leaving it
unnamed is how it would have spread.

**The suite's own coverage document is a list of what it can't reach.** The identity
review is the least-covered thing in the repository and it's the one human gate;
exercising it needs a real multi-hour extraction sitting at `awaiting_review`, not a
fixture. The multi-cluster fan-out and the split control have never run end to end,
because fragmentation is emergent over feature length — a 150-second trailer produced
4 proposals in 4 groups, so no source cheap enough to test on can reproduce the case
that matters.

**One track of 43 in the music library crashes analysis.**
`music_structure.best_windows` raises an `IndexError` on it. It's tracked as an
expected failure rather than folklore, and it's honest about the real problem: a track
that can't be analysed drops silently out of the qualifying set instead of surfacing
as a skip.

**What breaks at ten times the load** is the gate first, then the disk. Ten films is
ten review sessions and roughly 100 GB of clip libraries, and the pipeline has no
concept of a queue across films — one at a time, on one machine, with a person waiting
in the middle of each.
