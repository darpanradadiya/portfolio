## Context

A Streamlit dashboard over a Supercharger network: 50 stations and 25,000 charging
sessions drawn from every hour between January 2022 and December 2024. Five tabs, each
built around one operational question. About 310 lines of Python, of which 96 are the
generator that produced the data.

Built May 2025.

## The problem

A charging network fails in two directions at once. Under-provision a site and drivers
queue; over-provision it and ports sit idle against a fixed operating cost. Both are
invisible in a monthly total and only show up per station, per hour.

So the question the dashboard exists to answer is where the next port should go: which
sites are congested, which are carrying cost without volume, and which expansion buys
the most reduction in waiting.

## Constraints

**There is no real data, and there was never going to be.** Charging session data
isn't public, so the dataset is generated: `synthetic_data.py` builds 50 stations and
then samples 25,000 sessions from a three-year hourly range, with weather, events and
traffic volume attached. Everything downstream is therefore a demonstration of the
analysis, not a finding about Tesla.

**The consequence is that the patterns are the ones I put there.** A generator that
draws sessions from a distribution will show the distribution back. So the dashboard
can prove that the questions are answerable and the charts are readable; it cannot
discover anything.

## What I built

Five tabs, one per question. A utilisation map plotting session counts and average wait
per station geographically. A wait-time series with a 7-day rolling average, anomalies
flagged beyond two standard deviations, and event markers. A ranking of the ten busiest
stations. Revenue against operating cost per station, side by side, which is the
profitability view. And boxplots of queue length and idle ports, which is where the
over- and under-provisioned sites separate.

Streamlit, pandas and numpy for the reshaping, Plotly for the charts, Folium and pydeck
for the maps.

## Limitations

The dataset is generated, which is the whole story: no conclusion here transfers to a
real network, and the correlations between weather, events and demand are the ones the
generator was written to produce.

Beyond that, it's a read-only view over static parquet files. There's no ingestion, no
scheduled refresh, and no test suite. The expansion ranking is a chart to read rather
than a model that recommends, so a planner still supplies the judgement.
