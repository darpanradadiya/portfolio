## Context

A classification project on the UCI bank marketing dataset: 45,211 records of phone
calls from a Portuguese bank's deposit campaigns, 17 attributes, predicting whether a
client subscribed. Logistic regression, a decision tree and a random forest, each with
and without hyperparameter tuning.

Coursework, and the dataset is one a reader will recognise immediately, so the
interesting part isn't the models.

## The problem

The bank wants to know who to call. A campaign has a fixed budget of calls and most
people say no, so the value of a model here is entirely in finding the people who
would say yes.

## Constraints

**88.3% of the labels are "no".** That single fact governs everything. A model that
predicts "no" for every client is 88.3% accurate and completely useless, so accuracy
on this dataset carries almost no information. It is the metric the dataset is designed to
  make look good.

**Nothing was done about the imbalance.** No resampling, no SMOTE, no class weights. I checked the notebook and there's none. The models were trained on the distribution
as it comes, which is why the results look the way they do below.

## What I built

Outlier treatment by IQR, one-hot encoding for the ten categorical attributes,
standardisation of the seven numerical ones, then the three classifiers with
`RandomizedSearchCV` for tuning. Accuracy, precision, recall and F1 computed for
every model both tuned and untuned, which is the part I'd defend, because it's what
makes the next section possible.

## Results

The random forest was the best of the three: **90.15% accuracy, 66.13% precision,
37.58% recall, 47.93% F1.**

Read those in order and the project turns into an argument against its own headline.
The accuracy is 1.85 points above the do-nothing baseline of predicting "no" every
time. The recall says the model finds 37.58% of the people who actually subscribed. It misses nearly two thirds of the entire reason the bank commissioned a model. The
precision says that when it does flag someone, it's right about two times in three,
which is genuinely useful and is the only figure here I'd put in front of a
stakeholder.

Tuning barely moved anything, which I'd now read as a sign that the ceiling was set by
the class balance rather than by the hyperparameters.

If I redid this, the target would be recall at a fixed call budget: "of the 1,000 calls we can make, how many
subscribers do we reach". That's the question,
and accuracy was never a proxy for it.

## Limitations

Beyond the imbalance: the committed notebook doesn't run as-is. It imports a shared module that isn't in the repository, so the first cell errors and the reported figures
come from the README rather than from committed output. The dataset is public and
heavily used, so nothing here generalises to a bank that isn't this one in this decade.
And there's no cost model: a false negative and a false positive are treated as
equally bad, when a missed subscriber and a wasted call plainly aren't.
